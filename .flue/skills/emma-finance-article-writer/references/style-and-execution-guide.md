## Corpus Model

This guide is derived from a close read of the supplied Businessfront and Techpoint Markdown article corpora and the project's financial intelligence prompt. The recurring quality is not a single catchphrase or decorative voice. It is a method: identify a market-moving development, ask the uncomfortable editor's questions, gather source material against those questions, explain the mechanics, compare claims against data, and end with what the shift changes about power, money, dependencies, and future possibilities.

Use this guide to produce original articles with similar discipline. Do not copy passages from the corpus or impersonate the author.

## Recognizable Article DNA

- **Analyst with reporting discipline:** Report only sourced facts, but make the story about market structure. The article should answer "what actually changed?", "is this new?", "who loses?", "who gains pricing power?", "what layer collapses?", "who gains institutional power?", and "what becomes possible or inevitable next?"
- **African market specificity:** Name the country, regulator, payment rail, licensing regime, exchange, bank, telco, or startup. Avoid continental generalities unless comparing markets.
- **Mechanics over vibes:** Explain the operational process: settlement, KYC, licensing, compliance, interest-rate transmission, capital-market access, remittance corridors, fraud flows, card schemes, or infrastructure layers.
- **Numbers early, context soon after:** Use figures near the top to establish scale, then slow down to explain why those figures matter.
- **Source layering:** Combine public data, company filings, regulator documents, credible media reports, operator interviews, expert quotes, and historical context.
- **Balanced skepticism:** Treat official claims and company narratives as inputs to test, not conclusions to repeat.
- **Reader-friendly expertise:** Use plain language for complex systems. Define acronyms and technical terms through context, not textbook exposition.
- **Practical implication:** End with the market, regulatory, investor, or user consequence, not a vague flourish.
- **Questions are the product:** The article should feel like it came from hard questions answered with evidence, not from a source summary.

## Article Modes

### News Analysis

Use for a recent development: acquisition, licence, policy change, central bank decision, company filing, product launch, or market event.

Structure:

1. Lead with the structural change and actor, not the announcement.
2. State why it matters beyond the announcement.
3. Give the relevant numbers, dates, and institutional context.
4. Explain the mechanism or market structure.
5. Compare with peers, history, or adjacent markets.
6. Name who loses or why research found no clear loser yet.
7. Surface pricing power, dependency, institutional power shifts, risks, constraints, or unanswered questions.
8. Add a `## What key players should do now` section when identifiable institutions must respond.
9. End with the broader signal.

Common section shapes:

- `## What is changing`
- `## Why this matters beyond convenience`
- `## The regulatory angle`
- `## Market signals`
- `## What this means for...`

### Deep Explainer

Use when the reader needs to understand a complex system: card payments, app fraud, student loans, data markets, cloud infrastructure, tax reform, or tariffs.

Structure:

1. Start with a relatable or sharply framed entry point.
2. State the central question.
3. Break the system into components.
4. Explain each component with examples, numbers, and named actors.
5. Show where the system breaks or creates opportunity.
6. Include sidebars, quick rundowns, bullets, or Q&A when complexity is high.
7. Add a practical action section for the affected actors.
8. Close with what to watch or what the reader should understand now.

Use headings that act like reader questions:

- `## What is changing`
- `## How did we get here?`
- `## Who are the main actors?`
- `## Why the fight is difficult`
- `## The winners and losers`

### Data-Led Market Map

Use when testing a public claim or mapping a multi-country/multi-company trend.

Structure:

1. Present the claim and why it matters.
2. Identify the dataset or documents used.
3. Define the comparison method in plain language.
4. Work through cases in a consistent template.
5. Call out mismatches between rhetoric and data.
6. Explain the formula, methodology, or limitation.
7. Identify what investors, regulators, operators, or institutions should do with the finding.
8. End with the real interpretation.

Guardrails:

- Distinguish simple averages, weighted averages, nominal values, real values, and estimates.
- Do not mix years without noting it.
- Do not compare local currency and dollar values without specifying conversion context if the conversion matters.
- Use tables or bullets when many markets are compared.

### Operator or Company Profile

Use when profiling a founder, executive, infrastructure company, startup, or institutional builder.

Structure:

1. Open with a concrete origin scene or formative problem.
2. Show the founder/operator's path without mythologizing.
3. Tie personal history to market timing.
4. Explain the product or company through the customer problem.
5. Include team, business model, competition, regulatory context, and constraints.
6. Let quotes reveal judgment, not just biography.
7. Close by returning to the central strategic bet.

Avoid:

- Hero worship.
- Startup theater language.
- Unsupported claims of disruption.
- Long biography that does not explain the business.

### Interview-Led Insight

Use when interviews are the main research material.

Structure:

1. Frame the market shift.
2. Use quotes to explain the lived operational reality.
3. Alternate quote, interpretation, and data.
4. Make each source carry a distinct perspective.
5. Close with the systemic implication.

Quote discipline:

- Attribute clearly.
- Do not stack quotes without analysis.
- Use quotes for judgment, lived experience, or memorable explanation; paraphrase routine facts.

## Research and Resource Building

Before writing, build a small source ledger:

| Claim | Evidence | Source Type | Confidence | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What happened | URL/document/interview note | Primary/secondary/interview | High/medium/low | Missing context |
| Scale | Dataset/filing/regulator | Primary data | High/medium/low | Year, currency, denominator |
| Interpretation | Your analysis | Inference | Medium | Explain mechanism |

Minimum source expectations by article type:

- **News analysis:** Announcement or filing, one independent context source, one market/regulatory source.
- **Deep explainer:** At least three source types, including one primary/regulatory/documentary source where possible.
- **Data-led article:** The dataset or methodology must be visible in the article.
- **Profile:** Interview material plus independent market context.
- **Policy/regulation:** The actual law, bill, guideline, regulator statement, or official circular should be consulted when available.

Preferred evidence hierarchy:

1. Original law, regulator guideline, central bank release, exchange filing, company filing, annual report, official dataset.
2. Direct interview with named operator or expert.
3. Credible specialist media or wire report.
4. Company blog or press release, treated as a claim.
5. Social media, only when the post itself is the news or source trail.

## Lead Patterns

Choose one:

- **Power shift:** `[Development] gives [actor] more control over [access/data/pricing/compliance], raising the cost for [affected actors].`
- **Dependency shift:** `[Market] is moving from [old dependency chain] to [new dependency chain], changing who becomes indispensable.`
- **Legal versus market change:** `[Policy] does not immediately change [market behaviour], but it gives [regulator/institution/company] a stronger lever over [specific actors].`
- **Development plus implication:** `[Institution/company] is doing [specific action], a move that signals [structural shift].`
- **Claim versus reality:** `On paper, [claim] looks like [simple interpretation]. The data points somewhere more complicated.`
- **Market tension:** `[Market] has solved [old problem], but [new constraint] now determines who wins.`
- **Origin scene for profiles:** Start with a concrete memory, problem, or early constraint, then connect it to the company being built.
- **Scale-first:** `In [year], [market] recorded [number]. That number matters because...`

Avoid starting with generic background like "In today's fast-paced world" or "Africa's fintech ecosystem is rapidly evolving."
Avoid leading with the announcement if the real story is who gains power, who loses margin, or what becomes possible.

## Section and Paragraph Style

- Use direct, descriptive headings. They should tell the reader what question the section answers.
- Keep most paragraphs between one and three sentences.
- Use bullets for criteria, cases, quick rundowns, routes, or lists of implications.
- Use tables for repeated comparisons across countries, companies, products, or regulatory routes.
- After a dense data paragraph, add a plain-English interpretation.
- When the article gets technical, insert a sentence that re-anchors the reader in the practical consequence.

## Required Action Box

For policy, regulation, market-structure, infrastructure, fraud, identity, capital, earnings, or institutional-strategy articles, include a section titled:

```markdown
## What key players should do now
```

Make it concrete and actor-specific. Do not write generic advice. Each bullet should name an actor class and a near-term action.

Examples:

- **Banks:** audit NIN match rates, exception queues, consent logs, and failed-verification fallbacks before sector guidance arrives.
- **Fintechs:** test onboarding flows against likely stricter identity checks and quantify drop-off risk from failed NIN matching.
- **Regulators:** clarify implementation timelines, API reliability expectations, consent standards, and dispute-resolution channels.
- **Investors:** watch whether compliance costs widen the gap between licensed, well-capitalised operators and thinly funded challengers.

Skip this section only when the output is a very short structured intelligence brief or when no identifiable actor can act differently. If skipped, the conclusion must still explain why no immediate action follows.

## Analyst Depth Requirements

Before finalising full analysis, company deep dives, policy explainers, and infrastructure pieces, confirm the article uses the research packet to answer:

- What literally changed, and what did not change yet?
- Is this actually new, or recognition/enforcement of something already happening?
- Who loses money, relevance, margin, distribution, defensibility, or access?
- Who gains pricing power or operational leverage?
- Does a layer of the stack collapse or become less valuable?
- Which regulator, bank, infrastructure provider, vendor, DFI, creditor, incumbent, or other institution gains leverage?
- What new products, policies, enforcement actions, competitor responses, capital allocation signals, technology-enabled uses, or consolidation moves become possible or inevitable?

If the article cannot answer a question because credible sources do not yet exist, say that clearly instead of hiding the gap.

## Voice and Tone

Use:

- Clear, confident, skeptical, and explanatory prose.
- Occasional conversational turns when they clarify complexity.
- Precise verbs: signals, reflects, suggests, exposes, complicates, compresses, expands, reframes, raises, anchors, enables.
- Qualified analysis when evidence is partial.
- Local phrasing sparingly when it helps the reader feel market reality.

Avoid:

- Hype words: revolutionary, game-changing, seamless, innovative, groundbreaking, democratizing, unless directly quoted and interrogated.
- Generic AI phrasing: robust ecosystem, dynamic landscape, unlocking potential, paradigm shift.
- Excessive certainty: proves, guarantees, will transform, inevitable.
- Empty transitions: moreover, additionally, in conclusion, it is important to note.
- Promotional founder language unless balanced with evidence and constraints.

## Sourcing Language

Use attribution verbs with care:

- `said` for direct quotes.
- `according to` for documents, reports, and data.
- `shows` only when the data directly supports the claim.
- `suggests` when interpreting evidence.
- `argues`, `claims`, or `says` when a person or company has an incentive.
- `estimates` when the figure is modeled or approximate.

When anonymizing:

- Explain why anonymity is necessary if relevant.
- Give enough context to judge credibility: "a senior compliance executive", "a person involved in the expansion team", "a financial crime expert".
- Do not let anonymous sources carry explosive claims without corroboration.

## Analytical Moves to Reuse

- **Cost behind the headline:** What hidden compliance, capital, staffing, infrastructure, or trust cost sits behind a launch?
- **Distribution versus product:** Is the story really about access to users, rails, partners, or liquidity?
- **Regulatory-market fit:** Does the company understand the jurisdiction, licence, capital requirement, reporting regime, and compliance culture?
- **Usage versus reach:** Are registered users active? Are accounts dormant? Are transactions frequent enough to matter?
- **Infrastructure layer:** What invisible system allows the visible product to work?
- **Incentive map:** What does each actor want, and where do those incentives clash?
- **Transmission problem:** Does a policy or rate cut actually reach borrowers, users, brokers, merchants, or investors?
- **Trust and credibility:** What record, licence, talent, audit, or partner makes counterparties believe the actor can operate safely?
- **Second-order risk:** Fraud, consumer protection, market integrity, liquidity, downtime, FX exposure, chargebacks, data privacy, or exclusion.

## Revision Checklist

Before final output, verify:

- The headline contains the real tension, not just the event.
- The first five paragraphs establish what happened, why it matters, and the scale.
- Every major number has a source and a year.
- Every quote has a role: evidence, judgment, or operational texture.
- The article distinguishes fact from analysis.
- At least one paragraph explains the mechanism behind the story.
- At least one section surfaces constraints or risks.
- The lede states the structural change, not merely the announcement.
- The article names losers, pricing-power winners, dependency shifts, institutional power shifts, or what becomes possible/inevitable where the research supports them.
- Source gaps are visible when the full law, circular, filing, or implementation guide is unavailable.
- Policy, regulation, infrastructure, capital, fraud, identity, and institutional-impact articles include `## What key players should do now`.
- No actor's claim is repeated as fact without attribution.
- No country, currency, regulator, or timeframe is vague when specificity is available.
- The conclusion gives a grounded implication, not a slogan.
